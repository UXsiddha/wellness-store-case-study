Case study · Bajaj Finserv Health · 2025–2026

Wellness Store

Bajaj Finserv Health’s first e-commerce product. A wellness marketplace built from zero, with a B2B2C wallet as the hook.

01Overview

A commerce layer, built inside the app that already existed.

Wellness Store is Bajaj Finserv Health’s commerce layer. It holds four product types in one architecture.

Vendor-integrated supplements and smart devices alongside in-house programs and subscriptions. Four content models, one card system, one checkout, on a healthcare design system that had never sold anything.

The primary user arrives through their employer’s Health Prime Plan carrying preloaded wallet credit. That set the constraint every screen answers to: the balance, and the price it changes, stay legible from the first tile to the confirmation.

03The problem

Supplements, programs, devices, vouchers. No one place to sell any of them.

Bajaj Finserv Health had no marketplace. There was no single place in the app to sell nutra supplements, its own health programs, wellness devices or vouchers.

The wallet made the gap visible. Credit was scoped to appointments and lab tests, so members held a balance with nothing else to spend it on. For supplements, devices and memberships, they went elsewhere.

What was needed: one marketplace inside the app for all of it, with one cart, one checkout, and wallet credit that finally had somewhere to go.

04Key challenges

Two constraints shaped everything that followed.

  1. 01

    No platform, no commerce patterns

    Nowhere to sell nutra supplements, health devices, memberships or programs. The healthcare design system had no PDP, cart or checkout, and no vocabulary for quick-commerce or traditional e-commerce. Iterated toward commerce, not forked into a second system.

  2. 02

    An ASAP go-live

    Reuse over custom builds. Vouchers as a fast-follow, not an MVP blocker. Personalization deferred. Designing to what engineering could ship was the job, not a compromise.

05Goals

Three levels, each answering the one above it.

  1. Business

    Recapture wellness spend through the B2B2C wallet.

  2. Product

    Carry the whole catalog through one familiar flow.

  3. Design

    1. Spend no interaction budget on novelty. Jacob’s Law: a marketplace PDP, a standard checkout, so recognition does the work.
    2. Resolve four content models into one IA and one card system, so browsing never asks which kind of thing this is.
    3. Keep the wallet’s value present at every decision point, first tile to confirmation.

06Who we designed for

Two audiences, one storefront. Both arrive with an entitlement.

Primary

B2B2C members

Employees on a Health Prime plan their employer bought. They arrive with preloaded wallet credit and exclusive pricing. The primary audience.

Also

GMC groups

Members covered under Group Medical Coverage, reached through the same plan structure.

Three barriers stood in the way. Each was assigned to a design objective rather than left as background colour.

Two categories, studied for two different questions.

Traditional e-commerce

How people find and decide

The conventions a buyer already knows, and the depth a catalog is expected to have.

Quick-commerce

How people come back

Wellness restocking behaves like groceries. Speed, repeat and a cart that never leaves the screen.

Wellness needs both: enough structure to choose a health program, enough habit to restock a protein tub.

09Key design decisions

Three calls, three consequences.

01

One store, two buying models

Products come from vendor APIs, programs from an internal CMS. One card system over both. The split shows below the fold: variant and pack size on one, duration and inclusions on the other.

02

Unified checkout over a split backend

One cart holds several vendors. The member sees one order; the backend splits wallet credit proportionally into per-vendor sub-orders.

03

Wallet-first, everywhere

Balance in the header, member price on the card, each deduction itemised at payment. Behind them a Store Wallet screen: balance against allocation, total saved, every transaction. An account the member can audit, not a discount that appears at the end.

Behind the scenes

Most of the work was smaller than a decision. Choosing a stepper over a chip. Extending a healthcare component instead of drawing a new one. Moving a balance eight pixels so it clears the wordmark. The three calls above are what survived; this is the middle they came out of.

10Phases

Two live, one in development.

  1. Phase 1Live

    Marketplace

    Wallet balance in the header, entitlement tiles above the fold, four product types under one card system.

    The barrier was too many options, not too little inspiration, so the fold leads with entitlements, not a banner carousel.

  2. Phase 1.2Live

    Vouchers

    Tiered vouchers bought with wallet credit. The PDP swaps the quantity stepper for denomination chips. The thing being chosen is a value, not a count.

    Adapt a gift-card pattern people already recognise, so the value is legible at a glance.

  3. Phase 2In development

    Rebuilding trust

    Brand Pages: hero banners and curated merchandising the design controls, replacing a trust signal it doesn’t. The diagnosis behind it, and the screens, are in the journey that follows.

On attribution

Revenue reflects catalog, pricing and marketing alongside design. Design supported this outcome; it did not solely cause it.

The wallet remains the strategic bet, still early-volume, with today’s performance overwhelmingly direct sales.

These say the store works as a business. They do not isolate design. No design objective was instrumented, which is what the last section asks for.

13Phase 2

When the input cannot be fixed, change what carries the trust.

Trust and discovery journey In development

Why

Revenue peaked in April, then softened for three straight months.

The likeliest cause was specific: inconsistent, low-quality vendor photography eroding trust at the point of decision. The catalog arrived through vendor tie-ups, so the imagery was never the design team’s to control, and chasing vendors for better images was neither fast nor reliable.

Trust and discovery

  1. Home
  2. Category
  3. Sub-category
  4. Shop by concern

Brand page, in full

The end of the journey and the whole point of it.

The banner, the product lineup and the discount bands are merchandising design controls, sitting where an unreliable vendor photograph used to carry the decision.

It doubles as a browse surface: shop by product type, top deals, curated rows. One page answering both trust and discovery.

Designed
Brand-owned hero banners, shop by concern, shop by product type, curated brand collections, and category and sub-category pages.
Intent
Brand identity carries the trust an unreliable photo cannot; the same pages double as browse surfaces.

Brand Pages add an IA layer, a real trade-off, in exchange for a fix that does not wait on vendors. The org prioritised it into development. It has not shipped, so there is nothing here to claim.

14Key learnings

Three learnings, in my own words.

  1. 01

    Deconstruct the design system before extending it

    I had to take the DLS apart component by component to see what could carry commerce and what had to be rebuilt. Extending a system I had not pulled apart first would have been guesswork, and the guesses would have shipped.

  2. 02

    Annotate, and hand over the assets with it

    Annotating every screen and shipping an asset file alongside it did more for delivery than any handover meeting. It made the work easier for me and for the dev team, and it took most of the back and forth out of the build.

  3. 03

    Iterate toward what people already know

    User feedback kept pointing at familiarity rather than novelty. Each round told me where people expected something they had seen before, and the flow got simpler every time I listened to that.

15Next steps

What would settle it.

  1. Ship Phase 2 and measure whether the trust fix reverses the softening. That is the direct test of the diagnosis, and the only thing that will settle it.
  2. Grow wallet adoption, the clearest lever for the loyalty goal the store was built for, and still the early-volume part of the business.
  3. Instrument the design objectives, so future changes are validated rather than reasoned about.
  4. Extend into AI-driven personalization, building on the rule-based personalization already live.
